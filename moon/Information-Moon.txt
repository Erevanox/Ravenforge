## 1. RESEARCH METHODOLOGY AND SOURCE RELIABILITY

**1.1 Important limitations (read first)**
- This revised edition retains the original research as its starting point and adds a targeted source check against current NASA and ISRO mission pages. This is **not a complete independent verification of every technical value or URL**. Items not directly checked remain labelled approximate, uncertain, or requiring verification.
- Where I was not confident of a number, I wrote "approx.", "Not publicly confirmed" or "Unknown". Nothing was filled in by guesswork.
- **Before shipping:** open every URL, and confirm coordinates marked "Approximate" against the LROC or NASA documents.

**1.2 Source hierarchy**
1. Mission documentation and agency archives: NASA NSSDCA Master Catalog, the Apollo Lunar Surface Journal (ALSJ), Apollo Preliminary Science Reports, NASA LRO/LROC releases, ISRO, JAXA, ESA, Intuitive Machines, SpaceIL.
2. Peer-reviewed publications (cited by journal and volume where I am confident).
3. Wikipedia was used only as a secondary index to cross-check coordinates and dates. It is never the sole source for a key fact.

**1.3 Conventions**
- Dates and times are UTC unless stated. Local-time conversions are marked.
- Coordinates are decimal degrees: **latitude N positive, S negative; longitude E positive, W negative** (range −180 to +180). State the lunar reference system / convention when available. Do not infer accuracy from decimal places. A rover's last position must not be replaced with its lander's coordinates.
- **Location accuracy:**
  - *Exact* = identified in LRO/LROC or similar orbital imagery, good to tens of metres.
  - *Approximate* = from mission navigation or older catalogues, good to roughly 0.01°–1°.
  - *Unknown* = not publicly confirmed.
- Orbiters have **no fixed surface coordinates** (Rule 5). Where an orbiter ended in a deliberate or accidental impact, the impact site is listed separately as an event location.
- **Mission type vocabulary:** Orbital mission | Successful soft landing | Failed landing (crash) | Deliberate impact | Rover.
- **Timeline block (every record):** first presence at the Moon, last known presence, still present (Yes/No/Partly), and confidence (Confirmed/Uncertain). "Presence" means the physical object. "Operational life" is listed separately.

**1.4 Selection and co-location rule**
- The original 30 entries are **not all single physical objects**. Several are mission-level groupings that combine a lander, rover, orbiter, or impactor. Treat each entry as a research record, then create separate component objects in the application data model whenever the components have distinct coordinates, dates, or statuses.
- Examples requiring component-level separation in the app: Chandrayaan-1 orbiter vs. Moon Impact Probe; Chandrayaan-2 orbiter vs. Vikram lander/debris (Pragyan remained aboard and was not deployed); Chandrayaan-3 Vikram vs. Pragyan.
- Additions in this revision: Blue Ghost Mission 1 and IM-2 Athena (2025). Artemis II (2026) is included as a lunar flyby event only; it did not leave a surface object.

### Record 1: Luna 2

**Basic Information**
- Official name: Luna 2
- Alternative name: Lunik 2; "Second Soviet Cosmic Rocket"
- Object category: Impactor (hard impact; first human-made object to reach the Moon)
- Country: USSR
- Space agency: Soviet space programme (OKB-1 / Academy of Sciences)
- Mission name: Luna 2

**Historical Information**
- Launch date: 12 Sep 1959, ~06:39 UTC (Baikonur)
- Lunar arrival / landing / deployment date: 13 Sep 1959, ~21:02 UTC (impact)
- Mission completion date: 13 Sep 1959 (ended at impact)
- Historical significance: First human-made object to reach another celestial body.
- Mission objective: Reach the Moon directly; measure radiation, magnetic field and the interplanetary environment; deliver Soviet pennants.
- What happened during the mission: The probe flew a direct trajectory and transmitted until impact. Its instruments reported no significant lunar magnetic field and no radiation belt, and they supported the existence of the solar wind. This was a **hard impact, not a soft landing**.
- Current status: Impact debris on the surface. The precise crater has not, to my knowledge, been definitively identified in orbital imagery.

**Geographical Information**
- Lunar latitude: ≈29.1°N
- Lunar longitude: ≈0.0° (NSSDCA, approximate)
- Landing site / lunar region: Near Palus Putredinis, between Aristides, Archimedes and Autolycus
- Nearest recognizable crater: Autolycus / Aristides / Archimedes
- Location accuracy: **Approximate**

**Physical Information**
- Object dimensions: Spherical instrument container with antennae; exact dimensions not confirmed here.
- Object mass: ≈390 kg (reported)
- Description of the physical object: Spherical probe carrying scintillation and Geiger counters, magnetometers, micrometeorite detectors and ion traps. Metal pennants with the USSR emblem were reportedly on board.
- What remains on the Moon today: Fragments of the probe, and reportedly pennant pieces. The location of any upper-stage impact is not publicly confirmed.

**Timeline Compatibility**
- First known presence: 13 Sep 1959
- Last known presence: Debris assumed present to date
- Still physically present: Yes (as impact debris)
- Presence status: Confirmed (impact event); fragments not individually located. Location uncertain.

**Visual References**
- Image URL: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Luna_2 (landing page; verify)
- Original image source: Soviet space programme archive images, via Wikimedia Commons
- Image caption (suggested): "Luna 2 spherical probe replica; first human-made object to reach the Moon, 13 Sep 1959."
- Official source page: https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/nmc/spacecraft/display.action?id=1959-014A

**Research Sources**
- Source 1: NASA NSSDCA, Luna 2 (1959-014A)
- Source 2: NASA NSSDCA Lunar Impact/Landing list (nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov)
- Source 3: Gringauz et al. (1960), solar-wind ion-trap results from Soviet cosmic rockets (Soviet Physics Doklady) (VERIFY citation)

### Record 2: Luna 9

**Basic Information**
- Official name: Luna 9
- Alternative name: Lunik 9; Ye-6M No. 202
- Object category: Lander (first successful soft landing on the Moon)
- Country: USSR
- Space agency: Soviet space programme (OKB-1/Lavochkin)
- Mission name: Luna 9

**Historical Information**
- Launch date: 31 Jan 1966, ~11:41 UTC
- Lunar arrival / landing / deployment date: 3 Feb 1966, ~18:45 UTC (touchdown)
- Mission completion date: ~6 Feb 1966 (battery exhausted; last transmission ~22:55 UTC)
- Historical significance: First soft landing and first images from the lunar surface.
- Mission objective: Soft-land a capsule and transmit surface panoramas and radiation data.
- What happened during the mission: A ~100 kg landing capsule separated from the braking stage and bounced to rest. Its petals opened, an antenna deployed, and it returned the first lunar panoramas. These panoramas showed the surface could support a spacecraft, contrary to deep-dust fears.
- Current status: Inactive, on the surface.

**Geographical Information**
- Lunar latitude: 7.08°N
- Lunar longitude: −64.37° (295.63°E)
- Landing site: Oceanus Procellarum, west of Reiner and the Marius Hills
- Nearest recognizable crater: Reiner / Marius Hills region
- Location accuracy: **Approximate** (NSSDCA navigation value; an LRO-confirmed identification is not confirmed here)

**Physical Information**
- Object dimensions: Spherical capsule ≈0.6 m diameter (approx.)
- Object mass: Landing capsule ≈99 kg; total launched mass ≈1,538 kg
- Description of the physical object: Sphere with four petal-like covers, a TV camera, a radiation detector and antennae.
- What remains on the Moon today: The capsule, and the separated braking stage if it landed nearby (status unverified).

**Timeline Compatibility**
- First known presence: 3 Feb 1966
- Last known presence: Present to date
- Still physically present: Yes
- Presence status: Confirmed (landing), location approximate

**Visual References**
- Image URL: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Luna_9 (landing page; verify)
- Original image source: Soviet Academy of Sciences panorama archive, via Wikimedia Commons
- Image caption (suggested): "First panorama from the lunar surface, Luna 9, 3–6 Feb 1966, Oceanus Procellarum."
- Official source page: https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/nmc/spacecraft/display.action?id=1966-006A

**Research Sources**
- Source 1: NASA NSSDCA, Luna 9 (1966-006A)
- Source 2: NASA NSSDCA lunar landing coordinates list
- Source 3: Soviet Luna 9 mission descriptions (TASS, 1966), via NASA History and Roscosmos archives (VERIFY)

### Record 3: Surveyor 3

**Basic Information**
- Official name: Surveyor 3
- Alternative name: Surveyor C
- Object category: Lander
- Country: USA
- Space agency: NASA / JPL
- Mission name: Surveyor programme, mission 3

**Historical Information**
- Launch date: 17 Apr 1967, ~07:05 UTC
- Lunar arrival / landing / deployment date: 20 Apr 1967, ~00:04 UTC (after two bounces)
- Mission completion date: ~3 May 1967 (end of operations at lunar sunset; VERIFY exact)
- Historical significance: Soil-mechanics data for Apollo. It is the only robotic lander visited by humans: Apollo 12 astronauts Conrad and Bean visited it on 20 Nov 1969.
- Mission objective: Soft-land, image the surface, and dig trenches with a surface sampler.
- What happened during the mission: Surveyor 3 bounced twice and settled inside a subdued crater. It returned thousands of images and used its scoop to dig trenches, measuring soil bearing strength.
- Current status: Inactive, on the surface.

**Geographical Information**
- Lunar latitude: −3.0162°
- Lunar longitude: −23.4181°
- Landing site: Oceanus Procellarum / Mare Insularum
- Nearest recognizable crater: Surveyor Crater (~200 m, informal); near Lansberg and Copernicus ejecta
- Location accuracy: **Exact** (LRO-identified)

**Physical Information**
- Object dimensions: ≈3 m tall tripod
- Object mass: ≈295–300 kg on the surface (class value; exact vehicle figure not verified here)
- Description of the physical object: Three-legged, aluminium-frame spacecraft with a TV camera, scoop, solar panel and omnidirectional antenna.
- What remains on the Moon today: The lander minus the parts returned by Apollo 12. Removed were the TV camera, scoop, cabling and a piece of tubing. These were brought to Earth for engineering study. The remainder is still in place.

**Timeline Compatibility**
- First known presence: 20 Apr 1967
- Last known presence: Present to date. Components removed 20 Nov 1969 (partial).
- Still physically present: Partly (most of the structure)
- Presence status: Confirmed

**Visual References**
- Image URL: https://www.hq.nasa.gov/alsj/a12/images12.html (Apollo 12 image library with Surveyor 3 images; verify)
- Original image source: NASA / Apollo 12 Hasselblad magazine photographs
- Image caption (suggested): "Apollo 12's Pete Conrad inspecting Surveyor 3, 20 Nov 1969, ~160–180 m from Intrepid."
- Official source page: https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/nmc/spacecraft/display.action?id=1967-035A

**Research Sources**
- Source 1: NASA NSSDCA, Surveyor 3 (1967-035A)
- Source 2: NASA SP-146, "Surveyor III: A Preliminary Report" (1967)
- Source 3: LROC Apollo 12/Surveyor 3 site imagery (lroc.asu.edu)

**Note:** The 1970s claim that bacteria survived on the returned camera is widely regarded as contamination. It is not treated as a result here.

### Record 4: Apollo 11, Tranquility Base

**Basic Information**
- Official name: Apollo 11 Lunar Module LM-5 "Eagle" landing site
- Alternative name: Tranquility Base; Statio Tranquillitatis (IAU)
- Object category: Landing site (first crewed lunar landing)
- Country: USA
- Space agency: NASA
- Mission name: Apollo 11

**Historical Information**
- Launch date: 16 Jul 1969, 13:32 UTC
- Lunar arrival / landing / deployment date: 20 Jul 1969, 20:17 UTC (touchdown); first EVA step 21 Jul 02:56 UTC
- Mission completion date: Surface departure 21 Jul 17:54 UTC; splashdown 24 Jul 16:50 UTC
- Historical significance: First humans on the Moon.
- Mission objective: Crewed landing, return and sample collection.
- What happened during the mission: Armstrong and Aldrin spent ~21.6 hours on the surface, with a 2 h 31 min EVA. They deployed the EASEP package (seismometer and retroreflector) and collected ~21.5 kg of samples. The ascent stage then left. The descent stage became a launch platform and remains on the surface.
- Current status: Historic site. All hardware is inert. (Aldrin reported the flag was blown over by the ascent-engine exhaust. LRO images show shadows of the flag in later missions' views but not here; the flag's status is uncertain.)

**Geographical Information**
- Lunar latitude: 0.6741°N
- Lunar longitude: 23.4730°E
- Landing site: Southwestern Mare Tranquillitatis
- Nearest recognizable crater: Sabine; the tiny crater West (~180 m) was passed on descent
- Location accuracy: **Exact** (LRO-derived)

**Physical Information**
- Object dimensions: LM ≈7 m tall; ≈9.4 m across landing gear
- Object mass: LM-5 at launch ≈15,100 kg. Descent stage dry mass ≈2,000 kg (approx., verify)
- Description of the physical object: Gold-foil-wrapped descent stage and its landing gear. Left on the surface: the commemorative plaque, a silicon disc of goodwill messages, the Apollo 1 patch, medals, boots, tools, the television camera and equipment.
- What remains on the Moon today: Descent stage, plaque, PSEP and LRRR (LRRR is Record 5), and discarded equipment. The ascent stage left lunar orbit and crashed at an unknown location.

**Timeline Compatibility**
- First known presence: 20 Jul 1969 (human presence 20–21 Jul 1969)
- Last known presence: Present to date
- Still physically present: Yes
- Presence status: Confirmed

**Visual References**
- Image URL: https://www.hq.nasa.gov/alsj/a11/images11.html (verify)
- Original image source: NASA Apollo 11 Hasselblad frames; LROC site mosaics
- Image caption (suggested): "Aldrin on the surface, Mare Tranquillitatis, 21 Jul 1969 (NASA AS11-40-5903 series)."
- Official source page: https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/nmc/spacecraft/display.action?id=1969-059C

**Research Sources**
- Source 1: NASA Apollo Lunar Surface Journal, Apollo 11
- Source 2: NASA SP-214, Apollo 11 Preliminary Science Report
- Source 3: LROC Apollo 11 site images (lroc.asu.edu)

### Record 5: Apollo 11 Laser Ranging Retroreflector Array (LRRR)

**Basic Information**
- Official name: Lunar Laser Ranging Retroreflector (LRRR), Apollo 11
- Alternative name: Apollo 11 retroreflector; part of EASEP
- Object category: Scientific equipment (passive)
- Country: USA
- Space agency: NASA
- Mission name: Apollo 11 (Early Apollo Scientific Experiments Package)

**Historical Information**
- Launch date: 16 Jul 1969
- Lunar arrival / landing / deployment date: Deployed 21 Jul 1969 by Aldrin
- Mission completion date: No completion; still usable
- Historical significance: First lunar laser-ranging reflector. It established decades of centimetre-to-millimetre Earth–Moon distance measurements.
- Mission objective: Provide a target for Earth-based laser pulses to measure the Earth–Moon distance.
- What happened during the mission: Lick Observatory obtained the first returns on 1 Aug 1969. Measurements confirmed the Moon recedes at ~3.8 cm/yr and tested general relativity.
- Current status: Passive, on the surface. Its return efficiency has degraded, but it remains usable by observatories (for example APOLLO at Apache Point and OCA in France; VERIFY current users).

**Geographical Information**
- Lunar latitude: ≈0.6741°N (site)
- Lunar longitude: ≈23.4730°E (site)
- Landing site: Mare Tranquillitatis
- Nearest recognizable crater: Sabine
- Location accuracy: **Approximate** (placed at the LM site; offset ~tens of metres)

**Physical Information**
- Object dimensions: ≈46 cm square panel; 100 fused-silica corner cubes (3.8 cm each)
- Object mass: Not confirmed here
- Description of the physical object: Aluminium panel holding a 10×10 array of corner-cube prisms, aligned toward Earth.
- What remains on the Moon today: The complete array.

**Timeline Compatibility**
- First known presence: 21 Jul 1969
- Last known presence: Present to date
- Still physically present: Yes
- Presence status: Confirmed (active ranging returns)

**Visual References**
- Image URL: https://www.hq.nasa.gov/alsj/a11/images11.html (verify)
- Original image source: NASA Apollo 11 photograph
- Image caption (suggested): "Apollo 11 retroreflector array, deployed 21 Jul 1969; still used for lunar laser ranging."
- Official source page: https://www.hq.nasa.gov/alsj/ (ALSJ, Apollo 11 experiments)

**Research Sources**
- Source 1: Bender et al. (1973), "The Lunar Laser Ranging Experiment," *Science* 182, 229–238
- Source 2: NASA SP-214, Apollo 11 Preliminary Science Report
- Source 3: APOLLO project, Apache Point Observatory (physics.ucsd.edu/~tmurphy/apollo/)

### Record 6: Apollo 12, Ocean of Storms

**Basic Information**
- Official name: Apollo 12 Lunar Module LM-6 "Intrepid" landing site
- Alternative name: Statio Cognitum
- Object category: Landing site (crewed) with ALSEP
- Country: USA
- Space agency: NASA
- Mission name: Apollo 12

**Historical Information**
- Launch date: 14 Nov 1969, 16:22 UTC
- Lunar arrival / landing / deployment date: 19 Nov 1969, 06:54 UTC (touchdown)
- Mission completion date: Surface departure 20 Nov 14:25 UTC; splashdown 24 Nov
- Historical significance: First precision landing, ~160–180 m from Surveyor 3. First full ALSEP (long-term station).
- Mission objective: Precision landing, science station deployment, Surveyor 3 inspection and sampling.
- What happened during the mission: Conrad and Bean made two EVAs (~7.75 h total) and deployed ALSEP. It carried a seismometer, magnetometer, solar-wind spectrometer, suprathermal ion detector, cold-cathode gauge and an RTG (SNAP-27). They collected ~34 kg of samples and retrieved Surveyor 3 parts. The ascent stage was deliberately crashed, and its seismic signal was recorded.
- Current status: ALSEP shut down 30 Sep 1977 (all ALSEP stations). Hardware inert.

**Geographical Information**
- Lunar latitude: −3.0124°
- Lunar longitude: −23.4216°
- Landing site: Oceanus Procellarum (Mare Insularum)
- Nearest recognizable crater: Surveyor Crater (Surveyor 3); Lansberg
- Location accuracy: **Exact** (LRO-identified)

**Physical Information**
- Object dimensions: LM ≈7 m tall, ≈9.4 m across landing gear
- Object mass: Descent stage dry ≈2,000 kg (approx., verify)
- Description of the physical object: Descent stage, ALSEP instruments and RTG (the fuel cask was carried back to the LM), and scattered equipment.
- What remains on the Moon today: Descent stage, ALSEP, tools and Surveyor 3 (Record 3) nearby. The ascent stage impacted at ~3.94°S, 21.20°W (approx., NASA seismic location).

**Timeline Compatibility**
- First known presence: 19 Nov 1969
- Last known presence: Present to date
- Still physically present: Yes
- Presence status: Confirmed

**Visual References**
- Image URL: https://www.hq.nasa.gov/alsj/a12/images12.html (verify)
- Original image source: NASA Apollo 12 Hasselblad frames; LROC site images
- Image caption (suggested): "Apollo 12 Intrepid and ALSEP at the Ocean of Storms, with Surveyor 3 on the crater rim, Nov 1969."
- Official source page: https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/nmc/spacecraft/display.action?id=1969-099C

**Research Sources**
- Source 1: NASA Apollo Lunar Surface Journal, Apollo 12
- Source 2: NASA SP-235, Apollo 12 Preliminary Science Report

### Record 8: Lunokhod 1 (Luna 17)

**Basic Information**
- Official name: Lunokhod 1 (carried by Luna 17)
- Alternative name: Ye-8 No. 203; "Moonwalker"
- Object category: Rover (first remotely driven robotic rover on another world)
- Country: USSR
- Space agency: Soviet space programme (Lavochkin / VNIITransmash)
- Mission name: Luna 17

**Historical Information**
- Launch date: 10 Nov 1970, ~14:44 UTC
- Lunar arrival / landing / deployment date: Landing 17 Nov 1970, ~03:46 UTC; rover rolled off later that day
- Mission completion date: Last communication ~14 Sep 1971; end declared ~4 Oct 1971 (sources vary)
- Historical significance: First rover on another world.
- Mission objective: Remote driving, surface imaging, soil analysis, laser-reflector test.
- What happened during the mission: Lunokhod 1 operated for 11 lunar days and drove ~10.5 km. It returned ~20,000 TV images and ~200 panoramas, and made multiple soil analyses. When the isotope heater failed at the end of its life, it could not survive the night.
- Current status: Inactive. Its French-built retroreflector was rediscovered in 2010 (see below).

**Geographical Information**
- Lunar latitude: 38.33°N (final position)
- Lunar longitude: −35.01° (final position)
- Landing site: Mare Imbrium (Sinus Iridum area)
- Nearest recognizable crater: Maupertuis / Sinus Iridum margin (approx.)
- Location accuracy: **Exact** (LROC identification, 2010, ±~tens of m). The Luna 17 landing site, ~38.24°N, −35.00°, is approximate.

**Physical Information**
- Object dimensions: Eight-wheeled pressurised vehicle; exact dimensions not verified here
- Object mass: ≈756 kg
- Description of the physical object: Tub-shaped body with a solar-panel lid, eight wheels, TV cameras and an X-ray fluorescence spectrometer. Carries a French-built corner-cube laser retroreflector array.
- What remains on the Moon today: The rover and the Luna 17 descent stage.

**Timeline Compatibility**
- First known presence: 17 Nov 1970
- Last known presence: Present to date
- Still physically present: Yes
- Presence status: Confirmed (retroreflector ranged in 2010)

**Visual References**
- Image URL: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Lunokhod_1 (verify)
- Original image source: LROC (NASA/GSFC/Arizona State University) and Soviet archives
- Image caption (suggested): "Lunokhod 1 and tracks imaged from lunar orbit by LRO, 2010."
- Official source page: https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/nmc/spacecraft/display.action?id=1970-095A

**Research Sources**

### 8.5 Required corrections to the visualization data model

The original document uses some mission records that contain multiple physical components. The app should not map every mission record as a single marker. Use one `mission_id` with multiple `objects` where applicable.

Recommended fields:

- `record_id`: unique identifier for the physical object or mission event.
- `mission_id`: groups components belonging to one mission.
- `object_name`
- `object_type`: lander, rover, instrument, impact_debris, orbiter, flyby_event.
- `country_or_operator`
- `arrival_date`
- `landing_or_deployment_date`
- `operational_end_date`
- `last_confirmed_presence_date`
- `presence_start_date`
- `presence_end_date` (null when still believed present)
- `surface_presence`: true / false / uncertain.
- `latitude`, `longitude`: numeric values only when supported.
- `coordinate_status`: image_confirmed / mission_reported / approximate / unknown.
- `coordinate_reference`: datum / lunar reference convention, when known.
- `position_epoch`: date associated with a rover's coordinate.
- `orbital_period_start`, `orbital_period_end`: for orbiters.
- `status_as_of`: date on which the status was checked.
- `image_asset`, `image_source`, `image_credit`, `image_type`.
- `source_urls`: list of sources supporting the specific fields.

### 8.6 Correct timeline filtering rule

For a selected interval `[start_year, end_year]`, show a surface object if its physical-presence interval overlaps the selected interval:

`object.presence_start_date <= selected_end_date` AND (`object.presence_end_date` is null OR `object.presence_end_date >= selected_start_date`).

Do **not** filter surface objects by operational life. A failed, dormant or inactive lander can remain physically present long after it stops transmitting.

Use separate timeline tracks / layers:
1. **Surface heritage:** landers, rovers, instruments and impact debris.
2. **Orbital missions:** spacecraft while in lunar orbit; show orbit/trajectory only if reliable ephemeris data is available.
3. **Mission events:** flybys and other events that did not leave an object on the surface.

A mission's planned landing date is not an actual arrival date. Planned missions after the 2026 cutoff must not appear as completed historical objects.
